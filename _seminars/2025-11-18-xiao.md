## Abstract

Technology governance has long emphasized preventing harm and punishing wrongdoing, but far less attention has been paid to repair after harm occurs?or to embedding justice into the process of innovation itself. This talk presents a program of research on designing justice-oriented technologies, organized around two connected lines of work: deliberation and repair, and responsible innovation.

The first line examines how technologies can support deliberation and repair, helping people make sense of harm, negotiate responsibility, and build resilience. Drawing on studies of online harassment, misinformation, and AI bias, I show how systems like SnuggleSense and frameworks such as the AI Harm Reparation Taxonomy reimagine harm response as a collective process of sensemaking and accountability.

The second line extends this work toward responsible innovation, bridging my current and future research. Building on insights from harm reparation, I investigate how organizations can balance risks and benefits early in AI development. Through studies of AI decision-makers and the design of risk?benefit frameworks, I explore how responsibility can shift from reactive auditing to anticipatory and value-driven innovation.

Together, this research advances a vision of technologies that are not only technically robust but also socially accountable, fostering justice through deliberation, repair, and innovation.

## Speaker Bio

Sijia Xiao is a researcher in Human?Computer Interaction (HCI), Social Computing, and Responsible AI. Grounded in theories of social justice, sensemaking, and decision-making, her work develops frameworks and systems that make information infrastructures not only technically robust but also socially accountable. Her research has two goals. The first is to complement harm prevention with collective repair, examining how to mobilize diverse stakeholders to address impacts after harm occurs and support those affected through reparation and deliberation. She studies these processes in contexts such as online harassment, misinformation, and AI bias and risk. The second goal is to broaden Responsible AI's focus beyond harm to include benefits. She investigates how benefits are defined and weighed against risks in early-stage AI innovation and develops tools and frameworks that integrate Responsible AI principles throughout the innovation lifecycle. Her research appears in CHI, CSCW, TOCHI, and AIES, and has been cited by The Guardian, CNN, BBC, and The New York Times. She has received a CHI Honorable Mention and funding from the CMU?NIST Cooperative Research Center, the Berkeley AI Policy Hub, and the Center for Technology, Society and Policy.
